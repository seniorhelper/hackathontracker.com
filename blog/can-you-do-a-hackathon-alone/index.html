Can You Do a Hackathon Alone? A Solo Builder's Playbook

Short answer: yes, a lot of hackathons let you enter on your own. Plenty of people prefer it. You make every decision, you set the hours, and if you win, you do not split the prize. The trade-off is obvious too: fewer hands, fewer skills and nobody to catch your mistakes at 1 a.m.

This guide is about making that trade work in your favor. It covers how to confirm solo entries are allowed, which hackathons suit one person, how to scope a build you can finish, and how to present it so it looks like the work of a full team.

Step one: confirm solo entries are allowed

Open the official rules and search for “team”. You are looking for a line about team size. Common patterns are “teams of one to four”, “individuals or teams” and “maximum team size”. If the minimum is one, you are fine.

A few things to check while you are there:

  • Minimum team size. Some in-person events require teams of two or more. Better to know now.
  • One entry per person. Many rules allow only one submission per person. Going solo does not get around that.
  • Organizations. Some contests let a company enter. If you run a business, check whether entering as yourself or as the business changes eligibility or payment.
  • Team change deadlines. If you might bring someone in later, see when teams lock.

If the rules page does not mention team size at all, look for a help or FAQ page from the organizer, or ask in the event’s official channel. Don’t guess.

Pick events that suit one person

Some formats favor teams heavily. A 24-hour in-person event judged on ambition rewards a group that can split frontend, backend and pitch. A month-long online hackathon judged on a working demo and a short video rewards focus, which a solo builder has in abundance.

Good signs for solo entrants:

  • Long submission windows. Multi-week online hackathons let you spread the work across evenings and weekends. Browse online hackathons and sort by closing soonest to see how much runway each one has.
  • Category prizes. A “best use of” track tied to one product is often a narrower field than the grand prize, and it fits a tightly scoped idea.
  • Judging on a demo and video. If judges see a three-minute video and a short write-up, polish matters more than headcount.
  • A smaller field. Our best odds list ranks events by prize money per person signed up. Fewer competitors per dollar helps everyone, and a solo entrant most of all.

Some newer contests are built for individuals from the start. The Ship or Die $100K Hackathon’s official page says “one entry per person” and ranks entries by verified profit after 30 days, which rewards one founder who can build, launch and sell, rather than a large team.

Scope for one pair of hands

The biggest risk for a solo entrant isn’t losing. It’s not finishing. Every hour spent on a second feature is an hour not spent making the first one solid.

A scoping method that works:

  1. Write one sentence. “It helps [one kind of person] do [one job] in [less time / less money / less pain].” If you need the word “and”, cut something.
  2. List the demo path. The exact clicks a judge will see in your video, start to finish. Build only what is on that path.
  3. Buy, don’t build, the boring parts. Use hosted auth, templates, component libraries and the sponsor’s own SDK. Check the rules on what pre-existing code and tools are allowed.
  4. Set a feature freeze. Pick a date, well before the deadline, after which you only fix bugs, record the video and write the submission.

A small thing that works every time beats an ambitious thing that works sometimes. Judges notice both.

A sample solo schedule for a four-week hackathon

  • Week 1: Read the rules and judging criteria. Pick the idea and the one category you are aiming for. Build the ugliest possible version of the demo path end to end.
  • Week 2: Make the core path reliable. Use real data. Fix anything a judge could trip over.
  • Week 3: Design and copy. The landing screen, empty states, error messages and loading states. Ask two people to try it while you watch silently.
  • Week 4: Feature freeze. Record the video, write the submission, test every link in a private browser window, and submit at least a day early.

Add every deadline to your calendar so it can’t sneak up on you. Each listing on Hackathon Tracker has an Add to calendar button, and the calendar page has a feed you can subscribe to.

Cover the skills you don’t have

Nobody is great at everything. Solo builders get by with a few honest shortcuts:

  • Design: a clean template, one font, two colors, generous spacing. Consistency looks professional.
  • Writing: keep the submission plain and specific. Problem, who has it, what you built, how it works, what’s next.
  • Video: show the product working within the first 20 seconds. Our demo video guide covers this in detail.
  • Feedback: a friend, a forum or the event’s community channel. Ten minutes of someone else using your build will find problems you can’t see.

Present like a team

Judges score the submission. They don’t give points for headcount, and they rarely take points off for being small. What they reward is clarity: a problem they understand, a demo that works, and evidence you thought about who it is for. Our guide to how hackathons are judged breaks down what they look at.

Write in the first person or as “we”, whichever feels natural, but be accurate about who did what. If you used open-source code, templates or AI tools, follow the rules on disclosure.

When a team is the better call

Going solo isn’t always right. If the event is a short in-person sprint, if the judging rewards breadth, or if your idea needs a skill you can’t fake in the time, a team can be worth the split. Our guide to finding hackathon teammates covers how to find one, even remotely.

For everything else, one focused builder with a tight scope and a clear demo is a serious contender. Start by finding events that fit: hackathons open to everyone is a good place to begin if you are not a student.

FAQ

Can you do a hackathon alone?

Often, yes. Many hackathons allow teams of one, and the rules usually say so in the team size or eligibility section. Some events set a minimum team size, so check the official rules before you register.

Is it harder to win a hackathon solo?

It can be, because you have fewer hours and skills to draw on. But judges score the submission, not the headcount, and a small, finished, well-presented project often beats a bigger team's half-working one. Choosing the right event and scope matters more than team size.

What kind of hackathon is best for a solo entrant?

Online hackathons that run for several weeks suit solo builders best, because you can spread the work around a job and other commitments. Events judged on a working demo and a clear video are a better fit than weekend events that reward large, multi-part builds.

Do solo winners get the full prize?

If a solo entrant wins, the prize for that place normally goes to that one person, subject to the official rules on eligibility, paperwork and taxes. Team prizes are usually paid to the team to split, so going solo means no split.

Can I join a team later if I start alone?

Many events let you change your team until a set date, but some lock teams early. The rules or the event's help page will say when team changes close.
