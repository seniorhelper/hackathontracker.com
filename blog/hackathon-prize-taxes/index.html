Do You Pay Taxes on Hackathon Winnings? A Plain-English US Guide

Not tax advice. This is general information about US federal rules, based on IRS publications as of November 2026. It is not tax or legal advice. Rules change, states have their own, and your situation matters. If you win real money, talk to a qualified tax professional.

You won. The organizer emails a congratulations note, then a form that asks for your Social Security number. That second email is when many first-time winners start asking about taxes. The short answer for US residents is usually yes. The longer answer is mostly about paperwork, timing and a few edge cases that catch people out.

Prize money is income

The IRS is direct about this. Publication 525, Taxable and Nontaxable Income says prizes from contests and similar events must be included in your income, and its example reports a contest prize on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 8i, “Prizes and awards.” A hackathon cash prize fits that description.

Two other points from the same publication are worth knowing:

  • Non-cash prizes count. Prizes in goods or services are included at their fair market value. A laptop, a conference pass or a year of software is not free in the tax sense.
  • Refusing a prize is an option. Publication 525 says that if you refuse to accept a prize, you don’t include its value in income. That matters if the prize is an expensive item you do not want.

The official rules usually say the same thing in their own words. A typical Devpost-hosted hackathon, such as the WebMCP Challenge rules, states that winners are responsible for reporting and paying applicable taxes where they live, and that the sponsor may withhold part of a prize where the law requires it.

The forms you may see

Most hackathon tax paperwork is one of four forms.

  • Form W-9. US winners give the organizer their name and taxpayer identification number on Form W-9. Send it through the organizer’s secure portal, not as a plain email attachment.
  • Form 1099-MISC. The payer reports prizes in box 3, “Other income.” An IRS page on prize reporting says this applies to prizes aggregating $2,000 or more in a calendar year beginning after December 31, 2025, with the figure adjusted for inflation. Earlier years used a lower threshold. See About Form 1099-MISC for the current form and instructions.
  • Form W-8BEN and Form 1042-S. Winners who are not US persons usually provide a W-8 form instead of a W-9. The same IRS page says prizes paid to nonresident aliens are reported on Form 1042-S and are generally subject to 30 percent withholding. A tax treaty can change that; Publication 515 covers withholding on nonresidents.
  • Backup withholding. If you do not give a payer a correct taxpayer identification number, it may have to withhold. The IRS explains this in Topic no. 307, Backup withholding; the rate is 24 percent.

One trap: a prize below the reporting threshold may not produce a 1099, but that does not make it tax-free. The form is the organizer’s reporting duty. Your income is what you received.

Cash, credits, hardware and tokens

Hackathon prize lists mix very different things. Our guide to online hackathons with cash prizes explains how to separate cash from “prize value.” For taxes, the split looks like this:

  • Cash. The simplest case. The amount paid is the amount you report.
  • Hardware, passes and subscriptions. Goods and services are included at fair market value. Ask the organizer what value it plans to report, and keep a copy of the prize description from the official rules.
  • Cloud and platform credits. These are often listed with a big dollar figure. How a given credit should be valued and reported can depend on its terms, so this is a good question for a tax professional, especially if the stated value is large.
  • Crypto and tokens. Some web3 hackathons pay in tokens. The IRS has a separate digital assets page that explains how digital assets are treated. Record the date you received them and their value in US dollars at that time.

Team prizes

Team prizes are where most confusion starts. Read the official rules before you submit and look for who receives payment. Common setups:

  • Each member is paid separately. The organizer collects tax forms from every teammate and reports each share. This is the cleanest version for everyone.
  • One person is paid for the team. The organizer pays a team lead, who then splits the money. The full amount may be reported under that one person’s name. If that is you, talk to a tax professional about how to handle the shares you passed on, and keep records of every transfer.

Agree on the split, in writing, before the deadline. A team chat message with names and percentages beats a debate after the money lands.

Plan before payday

Most US winners receive the full prize with nothing withheld. That can feel great until April. A few habits help:

  1. Set aside part of it. How much depends on your bracket and your state. Putting a share in a separate account until you file avoids a surprise bill.
  2. Check estimated taxes. If you will owe a meaningful amount and nothing was withheld, you may need to make estimated payments. The IRS estimated taxes page explains who must pay and when.
  3. Remember your state. Most states have their own income tax rules. Check your state tax agency’s website.
  4. Keep records. Save the rules page, the winner notice, the payment confirmation and any form you receive. The rules page can change after the event, so save a PDF copy.
  5. Ask about costs. Whether you can deduct expenses such as software or cloud bills depends on your situation. A tax professional can tell you what applies.

Read the tax section before you enter

Taxes are another reason to read the official rules early. Look for the prize breakdown, who is paid, which forms are required, whether prizes can be paid to your country, and any withholding language. FAQ

Do you have to pay taxes on hackathon winnings in the US?

Generally, yes. The IRS treats prizes and awards as taxable income, and IRS Publication 525 says prizes in goods or services are included at their fair market value. Official hackathon rules usually say winners are responsible for their own taxes as well.

Will I get a 1099 for a hackathon prize?

Possibly. US organizers report prizes to the IRS on Form 1099-MISC, box 3, once a winner's prizes from that payer reach the reporting threshold for the year. The IRS says the threshold is $2,000 for calendar years beginning after December 31, 2025, adjusted for inflation. The income can still be taxable if no form arrives.

Why does a hackathon ask for a W-9 before paying my prize?

The organizer needs your taxpayer identification number to report the prize. If you do not provide it, the payer may have to apply backup withholding, which the IRS sets at 24 percent. Non-US winners usually fill out a W-8 form instead.

Are cloud credits, hardware or tokens won at a hackathon taxable?

Prizes in goods or services are generally included in income at their fair market value, according to IRS Publication 525. Digital assets have their own IRS guidance. Ask the organizer what value it plans to report, and check with a tax professional for credits or tokens.

How are team prizes taxed?

It depends on who the organizer pays and reports. Some organizers pay and report each team member separately, while others pay one person. If one person receives the whole prize and splits it, talk to a tax professional about how to report it before you file.

Is this tax advice?

No. This guide is general information about US federal rules as published by the IRS. Your situation, your state and your country of residence can change the answer, so speak with a qualified tax professional before you file.
