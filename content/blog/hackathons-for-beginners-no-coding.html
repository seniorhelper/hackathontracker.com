Many people assume hackathons are for programmers only. They are not. Teams need people who can pick the right problem, design a clear screen, talk to users, test the thing and explain it in three minutes. Those skills often decide who wins, because judges see the pitch and the demo before they ever see the code.

This guide is for adults and students who have never written code, or barely have. It covers how to choose a first event, what to check in the rules, which role to take, and a simple plan you can finish.

Pick an event that wants beginners

Not every hackathon is a good first one. Look for these signs:

  • A beginner-friendly tag. Organizers often label events this way. Our beginner-friendly hackathons page lists events carrying that tag, updated daily, and the main search has a Beginner filter.
  • Workshops, mentors or office hours. These mean someone is there to unblock you. Check the schedule or FAQ on the official site.
  • A long submission window. An online hackathon that runs for several weeks gives you time to learn as you go. The online hackathons list shows each deadline.
  • Broad judging criteria. Criteria such as impact, design, creativity or presentation reward skills that are not code. Our guide to how hackathons are judged shows how to read them.
  • Eligibility that fits you. Many events are for students. If you are not one, start with events open to everyone.

Read the rules for what you must submit

This is the step beginners skip, and it matters more for non-coders than anyone. The official rules say what a valid entry contains. Some are relaxed. Others are strict about code.

For example, the WebMCP Challenge rules require access to a working project for judging and a public code repository containing the source code, the instructions needed to run it and an open source license. A solo entrant with no coding skills would struggle to meet that alone. A team with one developer could.

When you read a rules page, look for:

  • Required materials. Video, description, working demo, repository, slides or a prototype.
  • Allowed tools. Whether no-code builders, templates or AI coding tools are permitted, and whether you must disclose them.
  • New work only. Many rules say the project must be created during the event. Don’t bring something you built earlier unless the rules allow it.
  • Team size. Whether you can enter alone, and the maximum team size.

If the rules are unclear, ask in the event’s official chat or forum before you start building. Organizers would rather answer a question than disqualify an entry.

Choose a role you can own

On a team, pick one job and do it well. A few that suit beginners:

  • Product lead. Defines the one user and the one problem, keeps the scope small and watches the clock.
  • Designer. Sketches the user flow and builds clean screens in a design tool. A polished prototype makes the demo credible.
  • Researcher. Talks to two or three people who have the problem and brings back quotes. Real evidence makes a stronger story.
  • Tester. Tries to break the project before the judges do, and writes down exact steps when something fails.
  • Pitch lead. Writes the summary and description, scripts and records the demo video, and handles the final submission checklist.

Our guide to finding hackathon teammates covers how to pitch yourself for one of these roles and where to find a team, even remotely.

Going solo without code

You can enter alone if the rules allow it and accept the kind of project you can make. A few honest points:

  • No-code and AI tools can get you far, but only use them where the rules permit. Some events welcome them, and some require hand-written source code.
  • Keep it tiny. One screen, one action, one clear result. A small thing that works beats a big thing that does not.
  • Show, don’t describe. Even a clickable prototype is easier for judges to understand than a long explanation.

A simple first-hackathon plan

Devpost’s own tips for planning your project suggest putting the deadline in your calendar and working backwards, starting a draft submission early, recording the demo about a week before the deadline and submitting at least a week early. For a beginner on a multi-week online event, that becomes:

  1. Week one: choose and confirm. Pick one event, read the rules, list the required materials and join or form a team.
  2. Week two: define and design. Write the one-sentence summary, sketch the screens and test the idea with a couple of real people.
  3. Week three: build the core. Your team builds the smallest version that works. You test, gather screenshots and start the written description.
  4. Week four: present and submit. Record the video, finish the write-up, check every requirement and submit early.

When it comes to the write-up, our guide to writing a Devpost submission goes field by field, and the demo video tips cover the recording.

What to expect from your first one

Set a goal you control. Finishing and submitting a complete entry is a real achievement, and many entries never get that far. Learning one tool, meeting one useful teammate or getting feedback from a mentor are all wins. Prizes are a bonus the first time around.

Also expect the jargon. You will hear words like API, repo, deploy and MVP. Ask what they mean. Hackathons that welcome beginners expect those questions, and asking them early saves time later.

Find your first event

Start with the beginner-friendly list, filter to online if you want time to learn, and open the official rules for any event that looks right. Add its deadline to the deadline calendar so it does not sneak up on you. Then pick a role, find a team, and build something small that works.
