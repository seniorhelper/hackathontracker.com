Online hackathons are everywhere. The good in-person ones near you are harder to spot. They are scattered across university pages, meetup groups, company event pages and student leagues, and plenty are only announced a few weeks out.

Here is a practical way to find hackathons near you, and a quick test for whether one is worth the drive or the flight.

Start with a search that knows where you are

The fastest first step is the in-person hackathons list. It shows every in-person and hybrid event we track, soonest first.

  • Type your city, state or country in the “City or country” box. Typing “Denver” or “Texas” or “India” filters the list to matching locations.
  • Press Near me to use your browser’s location, then pick 50, 100 or 250 miles. The distance math happens on your device and your location is never sent to us.
  • Try the nearest big city too. Near me only includes events whose city we can place on a map, so a quick search for the closest large city often turns up a few more.

Tap ☆ Save on anything that looks promising. Your saved list stays in your browser, and you can put all of those deadlines in your calendar at once from the calendar page.

Check the places that run local hackathons

No list catches everything. A few minutes on these sources fills the gaps:

  • Universities. Computer science departments and student clubs run a lot of weekend hackathons. Many are students only, but some welcome alumni, community members or anyone over 18.
  • Student leagues. College and high-school hackathon leagues publish seasons of events across many cities. On our cards these show a Students only or High school badge.
  • Local tech groups. Developer meetups, AI user groups, startup communities and coworking spaces often host one-day build events. Search your city name plus “hackathon” on the event and meetup platforms you already use.
  • Companies and cities. Banks, health systems, transit agencies and city governments run themed challenges, often with a local focus and fewer entrants.
  • Conferences. Big tech conferences sometimes run a hackathon alongside the main event. These can be open to anyone with a ticket, or apply-first.

Found one we are missing? Send us the official page. We add events by hand when the dates and prizes can be confirmed there.

Read the badges before you get excited

Local events have more fine print than online ones. On every card, look for:

  • Eligibility. Students only, High school and Invite only badges save you from events you cannot enter. Working adults can also browse hackathons without a student ID.
  • Dates. In-person hackathons usually run one to three days. The card shows the date range and how many days are left.
  • Registration. Popular local events fill up or close applications early. Where a source shows it, the card says whether registration is open.
  • Prizes and odds. In-person fields are smaller than global online ones, so prize per entrant can be surprisingly good. Here is how we work that out.

Is it worth the trip? A five-question test

A nearby hackathon is an easy yes. One that needs a flight and a hotel deserves a moment of math. Ask yourself:

  1. Can I actually enter? Check age, student status, country and employer rules on the official page first.
  2. What will it really cost? Travel, a room, food and time off work. Some events cover travel or offer reimbursements, so check.
  3. What is the field like? Compare the prize with the number of people signed up, and look at past winners if they are posted.
  4. Who will be in the room? Sponsors hiring in your field, mentors you want to meet, or a community you want to join can be worth more than the prize.
  5. Is the event real and well run? Look for a named organizer, published rules and a clear judging process. Our legitimacy checklist takes five minutes.

If two or three events pass the test, tick Compare on their cards to see dates, prizes, sign-ups and eligibility side by side.

No in-person events nearby?

It happens, especially outside big cities and university towns. Two good fallbacks: widen the distance to 250 miles and look for one strong event a season that is worth a weekend trip, or enter an online hackathon with a local friend and build together in the same room. You get most of the energy of an in-person event without the travel, and online events usually give you weeks rather than a weekend. If you would rather start something yourself, a library, coworking space or school can often host a small one-day build event.

Make the most of an in-person event

  • Arrive with a rough idea, not a finished plan. Talk to sponsors early. Their tracks often have fewer entries than the main prize.
  • Find teammates at the kickoff. Most in-person hackathons have a team-forming session. Our guide on finding hackathon teammates covers how to pitch yourself in thirty seconds.
  • Plan for the demo, not the code. Judging is often a quick table visit or a short stage slot. Know the one thing you want judges to see. How hackathons are judged explains the usual criteria.
  • Sleep a little. Tired teams make demo-day mistakes. A few hours of sleep usually beats a few more features.

Never miss the next one

Local events come and go fast, so let the calendar do the remembering.

  • Press Add to calendar on any card for a Google Calendar event or an Apple / Outlook file.
  • Subscribe to every deadline in Google, Apple or Outlook. It updates itself daily.
  • Follow the new listings feed to hear when fresh events appear.

Then check the in-person list once a week with your city typed in. New local events often show up two to six weeks before they happen, which is plenty of time to register, find a team and book the trip if it passes the test.
